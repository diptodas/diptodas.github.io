    <p class="research-kicker">Technology, power, and public life</p>
    <p class="research-deck">
      I study how artificial intelligence and digital platforms shape institutional accountability, social knowledge, and
      people's capacity to act.
    </p>
    <p class="research-intro-copy">
      My research moves across three connected sites: the institutions that govern AI, the methods used to evaluate it, and
      the communities in which technological systems acquire meaning and consequence.

      <p class="research-number">01</p>
      <div>
        <p class="research-label">Governing accountable AI</p>
        <h2>When transparency is not enough</h2>
      </div>
    </header>

    <div class="research-body">
      <p class="research-thesis">
        Making an algorithm visible does not necessarily make the institution using it accountable.
      </p>
      <p>
        Governments increasingly use AI in immigration, social services, education, and other public systems. My research
        follows these technologies through the institutions that authorize and operate them—from federal AI registers and
        impact assessments to visa triage and frontline services. This work asks what meaningful contestability requires:
        not only disclosure, but practical ways for people to question decisions and seek remedy.
      </p>

        <a href="{% link _projects/public_ai.md %}">AI registers and accountability</a>

  <section id="evaluating" class="research-theme">
    <header>
      <p class="research-number">02</p>
      <div>
        <p class="research-label">Evaluating and repairing AI</p>
        <h2>Who decides what “good AI” means?</h2>
      </div>
    </header>

    <div class="research-body">
      <p class="research-thesis">
        A model can score well while the categories it uses still misrecognize the people it claims to represent.
      </p>
      <p>
        Benchmarks and datasets carry assumptions about language, identity, harm, and whose judgments matter. I examine
        these assumptions in Bengali language models and in generative systems that represent religion, nationality,
        gender, sexuality, and caste. I also develop community-grounded approaches to evaluation that give people affected
        by online harm greater authority in defining both the problem and its repair.
      </p>
      <p class="research-paths">
        <a href="{% link _projects/AI_fairness.md %}">Identity and AI fairness</a>
        <a href="{% link _projects/minority.md %}">Community-grounded moderation</a>
      </p>

  <section id="situating" class="research-theme">
    <header>
      <p class="research-number">03</p>
      <div>
        <p class="research-label">Situating technology in social life</p>
        <h2>Platforms do not arrive in an empty world</h2>
      </div>
    </header>

    <div class="research-body">
      <p class="research-thesis">
        Technologies acquire meaning inside histories, relationships, borders, and inequalities that already exist.
      </p>
      <p>
        I study technology from the social worlds into which it enters: multilingual communities, religious minorities,
        migrant networks, creative economies, and publics shaped by colonial and national histories. Across South Asia and
        its diasporas, this work examines how people negotiate belonging, safety, and agency through platforms—and how they
        reshape the systems that seek to organize their lives.
      </p>
