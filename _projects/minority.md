> **Research Summary**
>
> The mechanisms used to govern online spaces, such as content moderation and reputation systems, frequently prioritize majority viewpoints while marginalizing religious, ethnic, and Indigenous minorities. This research examines what makes online spaces unsafe for these groups in the Global South, from hate speech and fear to surveillance during political movements, and develops pluralistic and restorative alternatives to punishment-based moderation.

---

## Why this research?

Minority users often experience harm online that platform policies were not designed to recognize: implicit erasure, misrepresentation, and fear rooted in local histories of violence rather than overt hostility. Surveillance, meanwhile, is exercised not only by states but laterally through peers and kin. Understanding these harms requires starting from minority communities' own interpretations of speech and safety.

Questions guiding this work include:

- How do intersectional identities shape users' experiences with hate speech and moderation?
- How do fear, surveillance, and social conformity silence minority voices online?
- What would moderation grounded in minority hermeneutics and restorative justice look like?

---

## Research Approach

- Semi-structured interviews with **religious minorities and Indigenous ethnic groups** in the Global South
- The **asynchronous remote community** method to engage distributed populations
- Co-creation of culturally grounded datasets and design of technical artifacts

---

## Research Outcomes and Contributions

- **The politics of fear:** Bangladeshi religious minorities' use of social media is shaped by fear tied to social conformity, misinformation, stigma, stereotypes, and South Asian postcolonial memory, which can push minority voices into a "spiral of silence." (See [CSCW 24](/assets/pdf/CSCW24_minority.pdf))

- **Usership:** Drawing an analogy to citizenship, we analyze the legal status, rights, membership, and participation of intersectionally marginalized users, such as Indigenous ethnic minorities and women, facing hate speech online. (See [ICTD 24](/assets/pdf/ICTD24_civics.pdf))

- **Lateral surveillance and resilience:** During Bangladesh's 2024 uprising, protesters and organizers experienced surveillance from peers, kin, and intimate ties as well as the state, and responded with improvised encryption, panic deletions, satire, and hidden speech. (See [USENIX Security 26](/assets/pdf/USENIX26_surveillance.pdf))

- **From punishment to restorative moderation:** We propose moving from punishment-based moderation toward restorative justice that addresses hermeneutical injustice, and demonstrate it with Mod-Guide, an LLM-based moderation feedback system grounded in Hindu and Chakma community knowledge. (See [COMPASS 26](/assets/pdf/COMPASS26_modguide.pdf), and [Community-grounded evaluation and repair]({% link _projects/evaluation_methods.md %}))

- **Indigeneity and stigma:** Related work conceptualizes Indigeneity in social computing and designs against the stigmatization of caregivers of autistic children in urban Bangladesh. (See [CSCW 23 Workshop](/assets/pdf/CSCW23_indigeneity.pdf), [ICTD 20](/assets/pdf/ICTD20_stigma.pdf))

- Safety, anonymity, and collective resilience in repressive contexts
