> **Research Summary**
>
> AI in public institutions rarely replaces decision makers outright. It reorganizes their work: how cases are triaged, how risk is scored, how data is collected, and how responsibility is shared. This research follows AI into high-stakes public services, including immigration, homelessness services, and higher education, to understand how human judgment, data practices, and fairness are shaped in deployment, and how people subject to these systems experience and contest them.

---

## Why this research?

Accountability for public-sector AI is often framed as a property of the model or of the documentation that describes it. In practice, outcomes depend on frontline workers' discretion, the data clients are willing or able to share, institutional post-processing decisions, and the resources available to act on a prediction. People affected by these systems, meanwhile, often encounter them only as opaque outcomes.

Questions guiding this work include:

- How do frontline workers use, adapt, and work around data-driven tools?
- How is human judgment redistributed through human-AI collaboration in administrative work?
- Where in a deployed pipeline do disparities emerge and compound?
- How do people make sense of, and respond to, opaque algorithmic decisions?

---

## Research Approach

- Ethnographic case studies of public-service data practices
- Replica-based fairness audits of deployed institutional ML pipelines
- Mixed-method analyses of institutional documents and public discourse
- Sociotechnical theories of bureaucracy, classification, and accountability

## Research Outcomes and Contributions

- **The datafication of care:** An ethnographic case study of the City of Toronto's homelessness system shows how standardized data practices coexist with frontline workers' heuristic decision-making under uncertainty, client reluctance to share information, and resource constraints. The temporality of client data limits the validity of predictive AI models in this setting. (See [CHI 25](/assets/pdf/CHI25_homelessness.pdf))

- **Asymmetries in algorithmic visa triage:** Comparing how Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada describes its visa triage system with how applicants discuss it online, we identify three asymmetries: epistemic (access to decision logic), jurisdictional (exposure shaped by geopolitical position), and temporal–relational (how waiting and uncertainty are experienced). (See [COMPASS 26](/assets/pdf/COMPASS26_ircc.pdf))

- **Fairness audits of deployed risk models:** A replica-based audit of a college's early warning system finds that younger, male, and international students are disproportionately flagged while older and female students with comparable risk are under-identified, and that post-processing into risk tiers amplifies these disparities. The work offers a replicable method for auditing institutional ML pipelines. (See [CanadianAI 26](/assets/pdf/CanadianAI26_fairness.pdf))
